Stabilization Points

How quickly minor-league hitting stats become trustworthy, and how Prospect Ranks uses that

A hitter's stat line is part skill and part luck. The stabilization point is the number of plate appearances at which a stat is half skill and half noise. After n PA, the share that is skill is n ÷ (n + stabilization point). Prospect Ranks uses these numbers to pull each stat toward league average before scoring a hitter: a stat that stabilizes at 400 PA, seen over 400 PA, keeps half of its distance from average; over 150 PA, about a quarter.

How they were found, three ways:

  • Noise math (about 39,000 hitter-seasons with 100+ of the stat's denominator): pure luck in n chances spreads a rate by a known amount. The spread among hitters beyond that is real skill, and the stabilization point is where the two are equal. This works for stats that are simple rates: K%, BB%, and the batted-ball rates.
  • First half vs. second half (13,333 hitters with 100+ PA at the same level in each half of a season, from the MLB Stats API): how well a hitter's first-half stat predicts his second-half stat. This covers ISO and wOBA too. It runs a little high, since some hitters really do change during a season.
  • Season to season (hitters with 250+ PA in consecutive seasons): this mixes real development in with the noise, so it only gives a ceiling. It's the only estimate available for Speed Score.

The values used are rounded from the first two methods where they agree. Level barely matters (K% stabilizes at about 50 PA in Low-A and 55 in Triple-A; BB% at 100 and 114), so one set is used for all four levels. The order matches the usual MLB rules of thumb: strikeouts settle fastest, then walks, then power, then results on balls in play (BABIP, about 850 PA, isn't a model input).

Does it help? Yes, modestly. Refitting the model with the regressed stats improved accuracy on players it wasn't trained on for all five outcomes (10+ WAR AUC .900 → .902, beating the old version in 88–98% of resamples). Halving or doubling every stabilization point gave nearly the same result. Each level's stats are regressed by that level's PA: regressing by the season's total PA tested worse, because a short stint at a new level says less than the same PA spent at one level.

How it's applied: both seasons the model uses are regressed, each by its own PA. A hitter who missed most of 2026 is scored from his regressed 2025 season, then adjusted for his limited 2026 hitting as the Prospect Ranks page describes.
